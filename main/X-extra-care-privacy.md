That's the point of this lesson. Most of the details that open your accounts, or tell a burglar your house is empty, are things people post themselves, cheerfully, in public. And the internet does not forget. Today we close some doors. Also we'll talk about AI chat assistants, which are clever, helpful, and occasionally wrong with total confidence. ## The Mender's way

Privacy means choosing who sees what about you. Here is how to post carefully, check what your apps can see, and use AI tools safely.

### What you post is public and permanent

- Assume anything you post online can be seen by **anyone**, and **kept forever**, even if you delete it later. Someone may already have taken a screenshot (a picture of the screen).
- A good test: would you be happy to see it pinned on the noticeboard in the local shop?

### Don't overshare

Keep these off social media:

| Don't post | Why |
|---|---|
| Your full **date of birth** | It's used to check who you are by banks and other services. |
| Your **address**, or photos showing your house number or car number plate | It tells strangers where you live. |
| **Holidays while you're away** | It tells burglars the house is empty. Post the photos when you're home. |
| Pets' names, first school, mother's maiden name | These are common answers to security questions. |
| Photos of tickets, boarding passes, bank cards or ID | They contain codes and numbers that can be misused. |

### Privacy settings on social media

Each app has privacy settings, usually under your **profile picture** or a menu of three lines, then **Settings & privacy** (Facebook) or **Settings** (most others). Look for:

1. **Who can see your posts**: choose **Friends** rather than **Public**.
2. **Who can find you** by your phone number or email.
3. **Location** or **tagging** options: turn off adding your location to posts.

Before each post, check the little word or icon near the **Post** button. A globe usually means **Public**.

### Location sharing

**Location sharing** lets chosen people see where your phone is on a map. It's handy for family, but check who has it.

1. Open **Google Maps** on your phone.
2. Tap your **profile picture** at the top right.
3. Tap **Location sharing**.
4. Check the list of people. Tap anyone you no longer want to share with, then tap **Stop**.

### Checking app permissions

A **permission** is something an app is allowed to use: your location, camera, microphone, contacts or photos. Check every few months that only the right apps have them.

### On Android

1. Open **Settings**.
2. Tap **Security & privacy**, then **Privacy controls** (on some phones just **Privacy**).
3. Tap **Privacy dashboard** to see which apps used your location, camera and microphone in the last day.
4. Go back and tap **Permission manager** to see each permission, and which apps have it.
5. Tap a permission (for example **Location**), tap an app that doesn't need it, and choose **Don't allow** (or **Ask every time**).

Samsung phones word this differently. If you can't find it, use the search bar at the top of **Settings** and type *Permission manager*.

### On Windows

1. Click **Start**, then **Settings**.
2. Click **Privacy & security** on the left.
3. Scroll down to **App permissions**.
4. Click **Location**, **Camera** or **Microphone** to see which apps can use them.
5. Switch **Off** any app that doesn't need it.

A weather app needs your location. A torch app does not.

### AI chat assistants

**AI chat assistants** (such as Microsoft **Copilot**, Google **Gemini**, ChatGPT or Claude) are programs you can type questions to in ordinary English, and they write an answer back.

They are good for:

- Explaining something in plain words ("what does this error message mean?").
- Helping you word a letter or email.
- Summarising a long piece of writing.

But:

1. **They can be confidently wrong.** They sound sure even when they've made something up. For anything important (health, money, the law, a menu name), check the answer with an official website or a person you trust.
2. **Never paste in passwords, bank details, codes, or private information** about you or others. What you type may be stored.
3. **They don't know you.** Use them to understand things, not to make big decisions for you.

### Deepfake voice and video scams

A **deepfake** is a fake video or voice, made by a computer, that looks and sounds like a real person. Scammers use a few seconds of someone's voice from social media to fake a phone call: "Dad, I've lost my phone, I need money urgently."

1. If a call or video from someone you know asks for money or codes, **hang up**.
2. Ring them back on the number you already have for them.
3. Agree a **family password** with close relatives: a word only you know, to ask for on any strange call.

## Do it now

1. On your phone, open **Permission manager** (see above).
2. Tap **Location**. Read the list of apps under **Allowed all the time** and **Allowed only while in use**.
3. For any app that doesn't need your location, set it to **Don't allow** or **Ask every time**.
4. Do the same for **Microphone**.
5. On the computer, open **Settings** → **Privacy & security** → **Location** and look through that list too.

You've done it when every app with your location or microphone is one you can say why it needs it.
