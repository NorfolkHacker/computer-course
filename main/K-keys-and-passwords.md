## The Operator says

Luser list, entry eleven: "But nobody would guess Psych123." Nobody guessed it. A pizza website lost it, and then a computer tried it on every other website in the world before lunch.

You need three things: a different password for every account, somewhere safe to keep them, and a second lock on the important ones. That's this lesson. Do it, and the next time a pizza website gets robbed, it's the pizza website's problem.

## The Mender's way

A **password** is the key to an account. Like keys, one per door, kept safely, and never handed to a stranger.

### Why one password everywhere is dangerous

Websites get broken into and their lists of passwords stolen. Criminals then try those same email addresses and passwords on banks, email and shops. If every account has the same password, one leak opens them all.

So the rule is: **every account gets its own password.** The most important ones to make unique are your **email** (because it can reset all the others) and your **bank**.

### Making a strong password: three random words

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre recommends **three random words** stuck together. For example: `TeapotGlacierTrumpet`.

1. Pick three words that have nothing to do with each other or with you. Not your pet, your town or your football team.
2. Join them together. Add a capital letter, a number or a symbol if the website asks for one: `TeapotGlacier7Trumpet!`

`Psych123` takes a computer seconds to guess; three random words take it centuries.

### A password manager: somewhere safe to keep them

A **password manager** is a safe, built into your browser or phone, that remembers your passwords for you, fills them in, and can make up strong new ones.

You probably already have one:

### On Windows

- **Google Chrome:** click the three dots **⋮** (top-right) → **Passwords and autofill** → **Google Password Manager**.
- **Microsoft Edge:** click the three dots **…** (top-right) → **Settings** → **Passwords**. If you can't see it, type `passwords` into the **Search settings** box at the top-left.
- When you sign in somewhere, the browser asks **Save password?** Click **Save**.
- When signing up, the browser may offer a **suggested strong password**. Accept it; it's remembered for you.

### On Android

- Your phone uses **Google Password Manager**, shared with Chrome on the computer if both use the same Google account.
- To see it: open **Settings**, tap the search bar at the top and type `password manager`. On a Pixel it's under **Passwords, passkeys & accounts**. Menus vary by phone maker.
- When an app asks you to sign in, tap the password box: your saved password is offered above the keyboard.

For one that works in every browser and phone, **Bitwarden** is a good free choice; get it only from `bitwarden.com` or the Play Store.

The password that opens your password manager (usually your **Google** or **Microsoft account** password) must be strong and used nowhere else.

### Two-factor authentication: a second lock

**Two-factor authentication** (**2FA**; Google calls it **2-Step Verification**) means that signing in needs your password **and** something else: usually a code sent to your phone, or a tap on your phone saying "Yes, it's me". A thief with your password still can't get in without your phone. The second step can be:

- A **text message** with a six-digit code.
- An **authenticator app** (**Google Authenticator** or **Microsoft Authenticator**) showing a new code every 30 seconds. Safer than texts.
- A **prompt** on your phone: "Are you trying to sign in?" with **Yes** and **No**.

Turn it on for your email, Google and Microsoft accounts, and bank. For your Google account:

1. In your browser, go to `myaccount.google.com` and sign in.
2. Click **Security** on the left (or tap the **Security** tab on the phone).
3. Click **2-Step Verification** and follow the steps.

For your Microsoft account, go to `account.microsoft.com`, click **Security**, and look for **two-step verification**.

### Never share a code

**Nobody genuine will ever ask you to read out a code sent to your phone**: not your bank, Microsoft, Google or "the delivery driver". If someone asks for a code, it's a scam (Lesson J). Equally, if you get a "Yes, it's me?" prompt you didn't start, tap **No**.

## Do it now

1. On the computer, open Chrome or Edge and find the saved passwords page using the steps above.
2. In Google Password Manager, click **Checkup** (in Edge, look for **Password Monitor** or **Password health**). It tells you about passwords that are reused, weak or leaked.
3. Don't fix everything today. Pick the one most important account it flags (email first) and change that password to three random words, saving it in the password manager.
4. Then turn on 2-Step Verification for your Google account.

You've done it when your email has a unique password saved in the password manager, and signing in to Google asks your phone for a second step.

## Quiz

```quiz
- q: You use the same password for a pizza website and your email. The pizza website is hacked. What's the danger?
  options:
    - None; it's only a pizza website
    - Criminals can try the same password on your email and get in
    - The pizza will arrive late
  answer: 1
  why: Leaked passwords get tried on other websites automatically. A unique password for each account stops one leak spreading.
- q: Which of these is the strongest password?
  options:
    - Psych123
    - TeapotGlacierTrumpet
    - Password1!
  answer: 1
  why: Three random words are long and hard to guess. Names with numbers, and the word password, are among the first things computers try.
- q: Someone rings saying they're from your bank and asks you to read out the code they've just texted you. What do you do?
  options:
    - Read it out; they're from the bank
    - Read out only half of it
    - Hang up; nobody genuine asks for those codes
  answer: 2
  why: The code is your second lock. Anyone asking for it is trying to get into your account.
```

## Scribble watch

The Scribble sends a "security alert" email saying your password has expired, with a handy link to change it. Real password changes are done by going to the website yourself, never through a link in an email. A password manager won't fill in your password on a fake website, because the address doesn't match. If it doesn't offer, stop.
