## The Operator says

Luser list, entry ten: two names, one parcel, no parcel. They're still up there. I wave sometimes.

Scammers aren't clever hackers. They're people with a script, sending millions of messages and waiting for one person in a hurry. Every trick here works the same way: make you scared or excited, then make you rush. Spot the rush and you beat nearly all of them.

## The Mender's way

Most scams follow one pattern. Learn it, and you don't need to remember every scam.

### The red flags

A message or call is probably a scam if it has:

- **Urgency**: "within 24 hours", "act now".
- **Fear or excitement**: a fine, a virus, a bank problem; or a prize, a refund, a bargain.
- **It asks for something private**: a password, card number, a code sent to your phone, or control of your computer.
- **It wants unusual payment**: gift cards, bank transfers to a "safe account", cryptocurrency.
- **It came out of the blue**: you weren't expecting it.

Real banks, police, HMRC and Microsoft **never** ask for your PIN or password, a texted code, or a move to a "safe account".

### Phishing emails and texts

**Phishing** (said "fishing") is a fake message pretending to be from someone you trust: "your parcel", "your account is locked", "a tax refund".

### On Windows (email in the browser)

1. Click the sender's name at the top of the email to see the full **address**, not just the name. `RoyalMail <delivery@rm-parcels-uk.xyz>` is not Royal Mail.
2. Rest the mouse pointer on a link **without clicking**. Its real destination appears bottom-left. If it doesn't match the company, don't click.

### On Android (texts and the Gmail app)

1. Look at who it's from. A "bank" texting from an ordinary mobile number (starting `07`) is a scam.
2. Don't tap links in unexpected texts.

The safe habit, on both: **don't use the link at all.** If it might be real, open the company's own app or website yourself. A real problem will show there.

### Fake virus pop-ups

A web page suddenly fills the screen with red warnings, an alarm, and "Your computer is infected! Call Microsoft Support on 0800...". It's a **fake**: just a web page. Your computer is fine. Microsoft never puts a phone number in a warning.

1. **Don't ring the number** or click anything on the page.
2. Close the browser with the **X** in the top-right corner. If it's full screen with no **X**, press <kbd>Esc</kbd> or <kbd>F11</kbd> first.
3. If it won't close, press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>Shift</kbd> + <kbd>Esc</kbd> to open **Task Manager**, click your browser (for example **Microsoft Edge**), then **End task**.
4. When you reopen the browser, if it offers to **Restore** pages, don't.

### Phone scams

A caller says they're from "Microsoft", "BT", "Amazon", your bank or the police. There's a problem, and you need to install a program, read out a code, or move your money.

1. **Hang up.** Microsoft and BT never ring to say your computer has a virus.
2. If you're worried it was really your bank, ring the number **on the back of your bank card**, or **159** (which connects you safely to your own bank from most UK phones). Use another phone or wait five minutes: scammers can hold the line open.

### If you've already clicked

It happens to careful people. Act quickly.

1. **If you typed bank or card details:** ring your bank on the number on your card (or 159) and tell them. They can stop the card.
2. **If you typed a password:** change it straight away on the real website, and anywhere else you used it (Lesson K).
3. **If you let someone control your computer:** switch off its Wi-Fi, ring your bank, and get trusted help before banking on it again.
4. **If you lost money:** report it to **Report Fraud** (it replaced Action Fraud in 2025) at `reportfraud.police.uk` or on **0300 123 2040**. In Scotland, ring Police Scotland on **101**.

### Reporting scams

- **Scam texts:** forward the text to **7726** (free; it spells "SPAM" on a keypad), then delete it.
- **Scam emails:** forward to **report@phishing.gov.uk**, then delete it.
- **In Gmail:** with the email open, click the three dots **⋮** next to **Reply**, then **Report phishing**.

## Do it now

1. Open your **spam** or **Junk Email** folder (Lesson I) and pick one email. Don't click its links or attachments.
2. Find the sender's full address. Does it match the company it claims to be?
3. Find two red flags from the list above.
4. Write the numbers **159** and **7726** on a sticky note and put it near your phone.

You've done it when you can name the red flags in one scam email, and the sticky note is up.

## Quiz

```quiz
- q: A text says your parcel needs a £1.45 redelivery fee within 24 hours. What should you do?
  options:
    - Tap the link and pay; it's only £1.45
    - Don't tap the link; forward the text to 7726 and delete it
    - Reply STOP to the text
  answer: 1
  why: It's a well-known scam to steal card details. Forwarding to 7726 reports it; replying just tells the scammer your number works.
- q: A web page shows red warnings and says to ring a number because your computer has a virus. What is it?
  options:
    - A real Microsoft warning; ring the number
    - A sign the computer is broken
    - A fake web page; close the browser
  answer: 2
  why: Real virus warnings never include a phone number. Close the browser, using Task Manager if it won't close.
- q: You typed your bank card details into a site you now think was fake. Who do you ring?
  options:
    - The number on the back of your bank card (or 159)
    - The number on the fake website
    - The number in the text that sent you there
  answer: 0
  why: Only the number on your card or 159 is sure to reach your real bank.
```

## Scribble watch

The Scribble's favourite word is "now". When a message, pop-up or caller says hurry, slow down and check by going to the company yourself: their app, their website typed in by you, or the number on your card. Nobody real minds waiting ten minutes.
