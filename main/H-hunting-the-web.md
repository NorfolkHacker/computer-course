## The Operator says

Luser list, entry five: a man who paid sixty pounds for a "checking service" to fill in a free form. He was very proud of how fast it went.

Search engines put whoever pays at the top. That's not a conspiracy; it's their business. The top result is often an advert, and some adverts are run by companies that charge you for things that are free, or cheap, on the real website. Learning to read a page of results, and to tell the real site from the one that's dressed up as it, is worth more money to you than anything else in this course. Possibly more than I'm paid. Which isn't hard.

## The Mender's way

A **search engine** is a website that finds other websites for you. The main ones are **Google** and **Bing**. Typing words into the address bar of Edge or Chrome searches with one of them automatically.

### Choosing your search words

1. Use a few **key words**, not a long sentence: `renew passport`, not `how do I go about renewing my passport please`.
2. Add a detail that narrows it down: a place (`bin collection Norwich`), a model number (`Brother HL-1210W manual`) or a year.
3. If you're dealing with the UK government, add `gov.uk`: `renew passport gov.uk`.
4. If the results are wrong, change the words and try again. That's normal, not failure.

### Reading the results page

Each result has three parts:

- The **website's name and address**, in small print at the top (such as `GOV.UK` and `www.gov.uk`). This tells you **whose site it is**. Read it first.
- The **title**, in larger, coloured text. Click this to visit the page.
- A short **description** underneath.

At the top, Google may also show an **AI Overview**: a summary written by a computer. It can be wrong. Use it as a hint, then check on a proper website.

### Spotting adverts

Companies pay to appear at the top (and sometimes the bottom) of the results. These are **adverts**, also called **sponsored results**.

- On **Google**, adverts have the word **Sponsored** above them.
- On **Bing**, they're marked **Sponsored** or **Ad**.

Adverts aren't always bad, but they're never the "best" answer; they're just whoever paid most. For anything official, **skip the adverts** and scroll down to the first normal result.

### Is this site trustworthy?

Before you type in anything personal or pay for anything, check:

1. **Look at the web address** in the address bar. Is it the organisation you meant? Scammers use look-alike addresses such as `passport-renewals-uk.com` or `gov-uk.help`.
2. **Know the official endings.** UK government services end in **.gov.uk** (for example `www.gov.uk`). The NHS is **.nhs.uk**. Universities often end in **.ac.uk**.
3. **Check what it wants.** A real service asks only for what it needs. Be wary of extra "handling", "checking" or "processing" fees.
4. **Look for who runs it.** A proper site says who it is, usually in small print at the very bottom of the page ("About us", "Contact").
5. **Check a second source.** If something sounds surprising, search for it on another well-known site, such as the BBC or the organisation's own website.

The padlock beside an address only means the connection is private. It doesn't mean the people at the other end are honest. Scam sites have padlocks too.

### On Windows

1. Open **Edge** and click in the address bar.
2. Type your key words and press <kbd>Enter</kbd>.
3. Read the small website address above each result before you click.

### On Android

1. Open **Chrome** (or tap the Google search bar on your home screen).
2. Tap the address bar and type your key words, then tap the arrow or **Go** key.
3. Read the small website address above each result before you tap. To see the full address of a page you're on, tap the address bar.

## Do it now

1. Open your browser and search for `renew passport`.
2. Find every result marked **Sponsored** (or **Ad**). Don't click them; just count them.
3. Scroll down to the first result whose address is **www.gov.uk**. Click it.
4. Look at the address bar and check it says `gov.uk`. Then close the tab. You don't need to renew anything.
5. Now search for `bin collection` and the name of your town. Find the result from your council (its address usually includes the town's name and ends in `.gov.uk`).

You've done it when you've found two official pages without clicking a single advert.

## Scribble watch

The Scribble's favourite costume is an official uniform: crowns, blue banners and words like "Service" and "Approved". Habit: for anything to do with passports, driving licences, tax, benefits or the NHS, **only use addresses ending in .gov.uk or .nhs.uk**. If a site asks for a fee you weren't expecting, stop, close the tab and go to `www.gov.uk` yourself by typing it into the address bar.
